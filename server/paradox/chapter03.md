## CHAPTER 3: PRIESTS OF THE NEW ORDER

**Jerusalem, Israel – Saturday, 12:01 AM**

---

The chamber had been waiting longer than the men inside it.

That was how Mattias understood the room — not as a space that had been constructed and then occupied, but as a space that had existed first and then arranged, over centuries, to have the right people standing in it at the right hour. The stone was Temple-era, quarried before the common era, fitted without mortar by hands that had been dead for two thousand years. The air held the particular stillness of a place that has never been touched by weather. The darkness at the chamber's edges was not the darkness of insufficient light but the darkness of depth — a darkness that went back further than the room itself, that suggested the room was only the outermost layer of something much older and much less bounded.

At the circular table, nine men waited.

Mattias was the tenth, and the youngest, and the only one still looking at the door.

The others had long since stopped watching the entrance. They sat with the patience of men who had learned to measure time in decades rather than minutes, whose waiting had long ago ceased to be anxious and had become instead a kind of occupation — purposeful, unhurried, indistinguishable from the waiting itself. Eight of the nine he had known for the three years since he had been brought into the council. The ninth he had met only twice, each time in different cities, each time at a meeting that had lasted no longer than it needed to. They did not use names here. They used the positions: the first, the second, the elder of the eastern line. Mattias was the youngest. He was called that.

He had a scar on his left palm.

He had gotten it at his first session with the sigils, three years ago, when he had not yet learned what careful meant in a room that contained them. The fourth disc had been closest to him that night, and he had reached across the table to adjust the angle of his monitoring equipment without looking at the distance between his unprotected hand and the sigil's surface. The contact lasted less than a second. What it left behind had never fully healed. Some nights it ached with a specificity that felt less like an old wound and more like a memory — as though the scar tissue were trying to remember something his conscious mind couldn't access.

Two men had died handling the sigils. A third had lost the use of his right hand from the wrist down. These were not rumors. Mattias had read the records. He knew the names, the dates, the precise nature of what had happened to each of them.

The seven discs sat on the table's center, arranged in a pattern he had spent years studying without arriving at a definitive interpretation. Each disc measured exactly twelve inches in diameter. Each was polished to a sheen that reflected nothing — the metal drank light rather than returning it, creating the persistent visual impression that the discs themselves were holes in the table's surface rather than objects resting on it. The sigils carved into their faces were not decorative. Mattias had understood that from the first night. The channels were too precise, the geometry too specific, the depth of each groove too deliberately calibrated. They were functional. They contained something.

The light inside them proved it.

The light was the element the records never adequately described. It moved. Not in the way that light in an enclosed space moves when the source shifts — not reflectively, not passively. It moved with the purposiveness of a thing that has intentions, pressing against the inner walls of its channels with what could only be described as effort. As though the carved grooves were not ornamentation but confinement. As though whatever generated the light had not been placed inside the discs so much as captured there.

Tonight, six of the seven sigils pulsed steadily.

The first disc was dim. Its light still present, still alive, but attenuated — reduced to a faint luminescence that moved slowly through its channels, the way a person moves through deep water. The first seal. Broken, now, on the other side of the world in the streets of Monteverde, in the trembling air above an Italian plaza where a physicist named Ethan Sharpe had watched the sky tear open and held a device that had gone silent and had then turned north.

Mattias knew Ethan Sharpe's name. He knew more than the name. It was his assignment, within the council's distribution of tasks, to know everything relevant about the Divinity Engine project — its architects, its methods, its failure, its aftermath. He had been monitoring Dr. Sharpe for fourteen months. He knew the path of the man's solitary tracking, the route from Rome to Monteverde, the signal the man had been following with the particular stubbornness of someone who has been told they are wrong but cannot make themselves believe it.

He had considered, on three separate occasions, whether to intervene. Whether to place something in Sharpe's path that would redirect him. Whether someone reasonable, watching from outside, had a responsibility to act.

He had not intervened. He had watched. He had filed his reports and attended his sessions and kept his scar covered, and he had told himself that someone reasonable needed to be inside this room.

The elder of the eastern line spoke first.

"She is late," he said.

This was the most any of them had said in forty minutes. The observation carried no irritation. It was simply information, placed into the room's careful silence the way a stone is placed on water — briefly disturbing the surface before the surface closed over it again.

"She arrives when she arrives," said the second, without looking up from the text open before him on the table. "She has always arrived when she arrives."

Mattias looked at the door.

He had read the descriptions. He had studied the accounts across four traditions and eleven centuries, each written in a different language, each reaching for the same figure from a different angle and never quite capturing her in the frame — always something escaping the edge of the image, some quality of the original that the description couldn't hold. He had approached those accounts with the disciplined skepticism of a scholar who had learned to distinguish between what a text claimed and what it documented, between the symbolic and the literal, between the archetype and the person.

He had been certain, until tonight, that she was the archetype. The symbol. The theological construct that accumulated around a particular human pattern across centuries of repetition — not a singular being but a recurring type, the same essential nature expressing itself through different women in different eras, each one recognized by those watching for the signs and recorded in the tradition as though she were continuous.

He had been certain of this.

He was no longer certain of anything.

The first thing that reached the chamber was the perfume.

Not the application of it — not the faint trace of a fragrance that clings to a person from proximity to something scented. This was the perfume itself, moving ahead of its source like an announcement, deliberate and rich and unashamed, filling the chamber's careful air with something warm and layered that had no business in a room this old and this austere. Amber. Something darker under it. Something older.

The oil lamps at the chamber's perimeter — four of them, their flames unwavering since the session began, as steady as the stone walls they illuminated — leaned.

All four, simultaneously, in the same direction.

Toward the door.

Mattias watched this happen. He watched the four flames incline themselves toward the entrance with the unhurried certainty of plants growing toward light, and he filed the observation in the part of his mind where he kept the things that defied the frameworks he'd been trained to apply, and he did not look away from the door.

She came through it as though arriving were an ordinary thing to do.

She was tall. She wore deep crimson — not a robe, not a gown, something that acknowledged the body rather than concealing it, that moved when she moved with the particular luxury of fabric that had never been cheap. Gold at her throat and wrists and fingers, layered and excessive in a way that was not accidental — the excess was the point, the statement, the language she spoke before she spoke in words. Her hair was dark and loose and she had done nothing to arrange it for the occasion, which told Mattias that she understood the occasion better than the men who had prepared for it.

She carried a cup. Stemmed, gold, old enough that its age had softened the metal's brightness to something closer to amber than yellow. She held it loosely, easily, at her side, with the carelessness of someone accustomed to carrying valuable things.

She looked at the ten men around the table with an expression that Mattias could not immediately categorize. Not amusement, though it contained elements of amusement. Not contempt, though it contained nothing of deference. Something more composed than either. The look of someone who has attended many versions of this meeting over a long period of time and finds each iteration both entirely predictable and faintly interesting for the particular ways in which it is also new.

Her eyes found Mattias last. They stayed on him for a moment longer than the others.

He looked away first.

"The first seal," she said. Her voice was low and carried the way the elder of the eastern line's voice carried — not loudly but completely, reaching every corner of the chamber without apparent effort. "You've been sitting here counting it."

"We have been preparing for the sequence," said the first. He was the oldest man in the room by a margin that was difficult to estimate. He spoke with the precision of someone for whom every word has been considered before the sentence begins. "The first seal confirms what we have been watching for. The hour requires—"

"Yes," she said. Not rudely. As someone says yes when they have already understood the rest of the sentence and has other things to attend to.

She walked to the table.

The motion the sigils made was not dramatic. It would have been easy to miss if you hadn't been watching. The light within all seven discs — including the diminished glow of the first — shifted. Not increased. Shifted. Moved from wherever it had been pressing against the channels' inner walls toward the side of each disc closest to her approach. Not randomly. In sequence, from the disc nearest to her to the disc farthest, the living light realigning itself the way iron filings realign toward a magnet. With purpose. With recognition.

The man sitting nearest the sigils — the third, a heavyset man whose right hand Mattias had never seen him use for anything, whose sleeve always fell to cover it — pushed his chair back from the table.

Six inches. A careful, deliberate six inches.

She noticed. The corners of her mouth moved in a way that was not quite a smile.

She was still three paces from the table when the first turned his head.

The motion was slight — barely a degree of inclination, the kind of signal that exists below the threshold of what most people would register as intentional. A glance toward the chamber entrance. A small downward tilt of his chin. He looked nowhere in particular for no longer than a breath, and his expression did not change.

But it was enough.

There were always guards stationed at the door. This was a condition of the chamber's operation that Mattias had never examined closely because it had always been a settled fact — the two men in the access corridor, changed every four hours, their post a formality he had walked past without considering its function for three years. He considered it now.

The door opened.

Two men entered. They moved with the practiced efficiency of security personnel operating on a received instruction, crossing the threshold in a way that suggested they had been told something specific about why they were being called in and were not inclined to ask follow-up questions. They came to a stop in the space between the door and the table, and the room remained exactly as still as it had been.

She did not turn around.

One of the guards moved toward her. He crossed the chamber floor with the bearing of a man performing a routine function — the professional neutrality of someone escorting a civilian out of a restricted space, neither aggressive nor uncertain, simply purposeful. His hand reached her arm.

The moment he touched her, his hand stopped being part of him.

That was the only way Mattias could describe it afterward, in the part of his mind that kept filing things because it had no other function to perform. The hand stopped being part of the man — stopped being connected to the system it belonged to — and for a fraction of a second the man himself simply stopped, every process halting simultaneously, the way a clock stops not when the spring runs down but when you remove the mechanism entirely. There was no recoil. No sound. No expression of pain, because pain requires time to travel from the body to the face and there was no time. There was the man standing upright, and then there was the man not standing upright, and the transition between those two states was so fast and so total that the body was already on the floor before any of the observers had processed the beginning of the fall.

Not dramatically. The way things drop when they have stopped being alive.

Mattias's scar burned. Not in warning — he understood this instantly, with the precision that comes from three years of learning to read the scar's particular vocabulary. Not warning. Recognition. The same signal the scar sent when he was close to the sigils. The same quality of acknowledgment, as though whatever lived inside the discs' carved channels had a counterpart somewhere in the world and the scar could feel both.

She carries what they carry, he thought. Or something older than it.

The second guard had taken one step back to the wall. He stayed there.

Nobody moved.

The body was on the floor. The first had not moved. His expression had not changed. He looked at a point somewhere on the table's surface with the composure of a man who has decided, in the span of a second, that he made an error in judgment and that acknowledging the error would require something he is not prepared to give. The elder of the eastern line had set his pen down. The second had closed the text before him, carefully, as though completing a thought.

No one looked at her directly. They looked at the sigils, at the table, at the grain of the stone beneath their chairs. The room had become a different room. The guard on the floor had made it so. The math of the chamber had changed: she was going to walk among them now, touching things, and the distance from her hand to the nearest sleeve had become the only geometry that mattered.

She turned from the table toward the body on the floor with no more expression than she had brought in through the door. She looked at it for a moment. Then she set her cup on the table and stood before the seven discs without touching them, her hands at her sides, and she looked down at them with an attention that Mattias could only describe as mutual. As though looking at them and being looked at by them were the same transaction, conducted simultaneously in both directions.

"You've kept them contained," she said.

"The channels were designed to—" the second began.

"I know what the channels were designed for." Her tone was not unkind. "I helped design them. The first time."

Silence.

The elder of the eastern line — who had, in Mattias's three years of observation, never been visibly unsettled by anything — set his pen down on the open text before him with a care that suggested he needed to put something down.

"The council has discussed the matter of custody," said the first, recovering his composure through the mechanism of formal language. "Given that the sequence has now begun. Given the nature of what is to follow."

"I know what is to follow."

"The sigils will be required—"

"I know what they will be required for."

The first paused. The particular pause of a man who has prepared remarks that the listener is not allowing him to deliver.

"The council has determined," he said, with the deliberateness of someone who has decided to complete the sentence regardless, "that you are the appropriate—"

"Yes," she said again.

She reached toward the nearest disc.

Mattias's scar burned again — a second pulse, precise and immediate, following the first with the logic of a system confirming a reading it has already taken. He looked at the man with the covered right hand. He had gone very still.

Her fingers stopped half an inch above the disc's surface.

The light inside it surged — not outward, not in the way of something trying to escape, but upward, toward the surface closest to her hand. Pressing against the boundary of the channel from the inside, reaching as high as the carved groove would allow, as close to her fingers as the metal would permit. A moth pressing against the interior surface of a jar toward the light on the other side.

She lowered her hand the remaining half inch.

Contact.

Nothing happened. Not in the way that nothing had happened when the second man had touched the fourth sigil briefly, wrongly, in a session two years ago and the light had moved outward from his contact point and he had pulled his hand back too slowly and the council had carried him to the surface and he had spent six weeks recovering the use of his arm.

Nothing happened in the sense that the disc simply sat on the table under her hand, and the light inside it ran through the channels with a fluidity it had never shown before, no longer pressing against the walls but moving through the designed pathways as though the pathways had been designed for exactly this motion — for this velocity, this ease, this particular quality of flow. As though the channels had always been accommodating something that would not arrive until now.

She lifted her hand.

The light in the disc continued its new motion for a moment. Then settled. Not back to its former configuration. Into something looser. Something that looked less like confinement and more like rest.

She looked at the six remaining discs.

"All of them?" she said.

"When the time is correct," said the first.

She looked at him. "The time is correct now."

The ten men exchanged no visible signal. But something passed through the room in the particular way that agreement passes through a group of people who have been together long enough that agreement doesn't require expression. The first dipped his head, once, by perhaps two degrees.

She moved around the table.

The man with the covered right hand moved his chair back. Not six inches this time. All the way to the wall — the chair's legs dragging against the stone with a sound that everyone heard and no one acknowledged. He stopped when the chair back met the stone. No one commented on this. No one looked at him.

She touched each disc in turn.

Mattias watched over the next four minutes as she moved around the table, and he found that the watching had become its own ordeal — the calculation he couldn't stop performing, the constant processing of the distance from her hand to the nearest sleeve, the angle of her body relative to the man she was passing, the geometry of how close was close. Every man at the table was doing it. He could feel it happening collectively, the private arithmetic of ten men trying to appear unoccupied while monitoring every inch of movement in the room. Nobody at the table had looked at the body on the floor since the first few seconds. The body was present in the room the way an unlit fuse is present — not acting, but changing what everything else around it meant.

She passed the elder of the eastern line. She passed the second. She moved with no apparent awareness of the specific terror she generated in each six-foot radius she entered, or she was aware of it in the way she seemed to be aware of everything — completely, without reaction, as data that required no response.

When she reached Mattias she stopped.

He had known she would. He had known it the way he had known things tonight that he had no framework for knowing — the scar informing him ahead of his understanding, ahead of his ability to prepare. She stood beside him and she looked at him with that expression of complete attention, the expression she had given the discs before touching each of them. Not the same as the look she gave the other men. More specific. More considered.

He was the most afraid in the room. He knew this with a clarity that left no room for the management of it. He had seen the guard touch her arm. He had seen what the contact produced. He had a scar on his palm that burned with recognition every time she came near one of the discs, and she was standing three feet from him now, and everything he knew about what she carried told him that the distance was irrelevant, that close was close, that there was no particular reason he should expect a different outcome than the guard.

She reached out.

She touched his scarred palm. Deliberately, with the same ease she had touched the discs — not accidental contact, not a brush, but a considered placement of her hand against the specific geography of the old wound. As though she had known exactly where it was.

He did not die.

He felt something. He spent the rest of the night trying to name it and arrived only at what it was not: not pain, not electricity, not the heat of the scar's familiar burning. Something beneath or before those sensations — the quality of being recognized, perhaps, by something that has been looking for a long time. Or warmth, simple and directionless, with no obvious origin. Or nothing at all, which was somehow the most disturbing of the possibilities, because nothing at all meant the absence of the thing he had been braced for, and the absence of the expected thing left a space that his mind immediately moved to fill with everything he didn't understand.

She held his gaze for a moment.

Then she moved on without explanation, and the disc nearest him received her touch, and its light settled into the same new configuration as the others.

By the time she reached the seventh disc, all seven sigils had been touched, all seven lights running through their channels with that new looseness, that quality of rest that was also readiness.

She straightened. She looked at the table — all seven sigils, all seven shifted into their new configuration, all seven lights running through their channels with that new looseness, that quality of rest that was also readiness.

She picked up her cup.

There was no discussion. There was no vote, no formal decision, no procedural moment at which the council as a body could be said to have ratified what was happening. Discussion would have required someone to indicate they intended to oppose her, and indicating that now — in a room where a man lay on the floor who had not been alive for four minutes — required a specific kind of courage that was not available in this chamber tonight. The first did not speak. The second did not speak. The elder of the eastern line looked at the text in front of him and did not speak.

She had not come here to receive the sigils as a gift. Mattias understood this only now, watching her hold the cup and look at the discs with an expression that belonged on someone reviewing the final items on a list they had been carrying for a very long time. She had known what they were for. She had always known. She had been waiting — with the patience of someone who measures time in a different unit than the people around her — for exactly this sequence of events to produce exactly this room: a dead guard, a diminished first seal, ten men sitting very still, and seven discs whose light was finally running the way she had designed it to run.

Not receiving. Collecting. The logic of the claim was older than any protocol the council had ever written and predated any authority they had ever held, and she had simply waited for the moment when the truth of that would be legible to everyone present.

"When they're needed," she said, "I'll know."

"There are protocols," said the second. "For the deployment of each—"

"There are old protocols," she said. Her voice carried no edge. It was simply a correction. "Written by people who understood half of what they were writing about. I was there when they wrote them." She considered. "I was there when they wrote the first versions. The ones the later versions were based on."

The second looked at the first. The first's expression did not change.

She moved toward the door.

At the threshold she paused, not turning, her profile barely visible in the oil lamp's leaning light.

"He's moving," she said. "The one you've been watching."

Mattias felt it before he understood it — the specific quality of being addressed that precedes the recognition that you are the one being spoken to.

"The physicist," she continued. "He turned north tonight. He's coming to Rome." A pause. "You should let him come."

She went through the door.

The oil lamps straightened.

Their flames returned to vertical as though they had never leaned, settling into the ordinary steadiness they'd maintained for the previous four hours with no indication that they had done anything else.

Mattias sat with his scar pressed against his palm and his eyes on the seven discs on the table, on the light moving through their channels with that new quality of ease, and he thought about a word the ancient texts used for her — a word that appeared in seventeen languages across thirty-two centuries of documentation, always the same word, always reaching for the same thing.

*Mystery.*

Not as a quality of her nature but as her designation. Her title. The thing she was called by the ones who had tried to name what she was and had arrived, after centuries of attempting, at the acknowledgment that naming her required the same word as acknowledging the limits of naming itself.

He was thirty-four years old. He had been in this council for three years. He had a scar that burned when the sigils were active and a file on a physicist moving north through the darkness of a world that had just lost its lights.

He should tell someone about the physicist. He knew this. It was his function, his assignment, the reason the council had placed him in this role. He should send the report. He should flag the movement.

He had considered, on three separate occasions, whether to intervene in Sharpe's path.

He was considering it for the fourth time now, and finding that the question had changed shape on him — that it was no longer the same question it had been before tonight, before she had stood at this table and touched the discs and the light inside them had recognized her and moved toward her like something long confined finally finding what it had been waiting for.

He was no longer certain that Sharpe was the one whose path needed redirecting.

The lights went out.

Not the oil lamps — those guttered but held, their small flames surviving on physical fuel rather than electrical infrastructure. Everything else: the security lights embedded in the chamber's ceiling, the emergency indicators on the access corridor's walls, the faint digital readout of the monitoring equipment the council's technical staff had installed in the chamber's anteroom and never fully integrated with the ancient stone around it.

Gone. All of it. Simultaneously.

The way things go out when a switch is thrown rather than when they fail.

One of the nine men produced a phone from his jacket. Its screen lit his face briefly — and then the screen went dark too, the device's connection to any network or satellite simply absent, as though the network had ceased to be rather than merely become unavailable.

Battery, Mattias thought. The battery-powered lights still worked. Someone had said this. He couldn't remember where he had heard it tonight, whether it was something the council's communications staff had reported or something he had read or something that had moved through the room the way her perfume had moved through it, arriving before its source.

He knew what had done this. He knew the scale of it — that it was not Jerusalem, not a local failure, not an infrastructure problem specific to this district or this country. He had spent enough time with the council's monitoring apparatus to understand what a worldwide simultaneous grid failure would look like on their instruments and to recognize it when it arrived.

In the oil lamp's surviving light, the seven discs sat on the table. Their light moved through the channels with that new looseness. That quality between confinement and freedom that had no name in any of the frameworks he had.

She had said she would know when they were needed.

He looked at the nearest disc. The light inside it ran through its channel with an ease it had never shown in three years of his attendance — fluid, unforced, the motion of something that had been arranged wrongly for a long time and had finally, in the last four minutes, been arranged correctly.

The darkness around the oil lamps was absolute. The darkness of a world with the electricity removed from it. Forty feet above the chamber's ceiling, Jerusalem moved through a night without streetlights, without the glow of phones or screens, without the ordinary ambient brightness that modern cities generate without thinking about it. Older darkness than any of the living had experienced. The darkness of centuries of nights before the mechanisms that defeated darkness were invented.

She was out there somewhere in it.

Not afraid of it. He was certain of that, with the specific certainty of someone who has understood a thing without reasoning to it. She was out there in the darkness that had just descended on the entire world, and she was not afraid of it because she did not experience darkness the way the rest of them did — not as absence, not as deprivation, not as the removal of something necessary.

For her, Mattias thought, darkness was simply the other condition. The one that had existed before the light was called into being. The one that remained underneath the light at all times, patient, waiting.

He thought of the physicist moving north through the dark Italian roads. He thought of what the man was moving toward without knowing it. He thought of what was moving in the same direction, somewhere in the lit corridors of a ruined Vatican laboratory, wearing a face that two billion people recognized.

He thought of the seven discs and the light inside them and the woman who had walked out of this chamber carrying nothing he could see but taking everything that had been sitting at the center of this table for the three years he had spent in this room telling himself someone reasonable needed to be here.

He pressed his scarred palm flat against the table.

"What have we done?" he said.

He had not meant to say it aloud. No one answered. The nine men around him sat in the oil lamp's light with the patience of men who had learned to measure time in decades, and none of them looked at him, and in the silence it became clear that the question was one they had either already answered for themselves or had decided they could not afford to ask.

The light in the nearest sigil moved through its channel. Fluid. Unimpeded. Ready.

In the darkness above Jerusalem, somewhere between the chamber's ceiling and the lightless sky, something ancient and patient and dressed in crimson was walking toward whatever came next.
