## CHAPTER 4: WHEN THE NUMBERS RUN OUT

**Florence, Italy – Saturday, 2:13 AM**

---

In a single, shuddering breath, the world came undone. Panic gave way to collapse, collapse to darkness—and at the eye of the storm, even Heaven wept.

The markets fell first, crashing in relentless waves that dragged entire nations into the abyss. Then came the riots, fires devouring city blocks while chaos tore apart civilization's fragile threads. Finally, the killing blow: a global blackout that plunged humanity's greatest metropolises into suffocating darkness.

Yet here, in Father Elijah's study within the Monastery of San Marco, an ancient stillness endured. The room bore the weight of centuries—its stone walls had witnessed the rise and fall of empires, sheltered secrets that could shatter faith or forge it anew. Tonight, those walls seemed to pulse with an otherworldly awareness.

Elijah sat at his desk, his seventy-three-year-old frame bearing the burden of prophecies no mortal should have to carry. Candlelight caught the deep lines etched in his face, each one a testament to decades spent wrestling with divine mysteries. His hands—weathered as ancient parchment—traced the edges of a manuscript that predated the Renaissance.

The heavy downpour outside battered the monastery walls like a dirge. Fat droplets cascaded down stained glass windows that depicted the Book of Revelation's seven seals. Through the distorted glass, lightning transformed the historic cityscape into a tableau of twisted shadows. The air hung thick with the scent of melting beeswax, rain-soaked stone, and something else—something ancient and unsettling.

Then came the whispers.

They started soft, threading through the storm's chorus like ghostly breath against the flickering flames. These weren't the desperate prayers of a world unraveling beyond the monastery walls. No, these whispers carried the weight of prophecy, the inexorable truth of divine revelation.

"The first seal is broken."

The words struck Elijah's soul like physical blows. His quill trembled above parchment, ink falling in dark tears that bloomed across the page. Memory crashed through him—visions received in the depths of prayer twenty years ago, when God first marked him as a witness to the end times.

"Verbum Domini," he whispered, the Latin prayer catching in his throat. "God's word remains eternal, even as the world crumbles."

His eyes fell to the ancient text before him—the Codex Prophecium, a ninth-century manuscript whose warnings had haunted him since his discovery of it in the Vatican's secret archives. Its words, written in a mixture of Latin and forgotten languages, seemed to writhe on the page:

*"Beware the one who comes in light yet speaks with a serpent's tongue. He will gather nations under his banner, but his throne shall be built on lies. When the first seal breaks, the deceiver will walk in flesh, wearing grace like a cloak to hide the void within."*

Thunder cracked overhead with bone-rattling force. The candle's flame danced wildly, casting twisted shadows that seemed to move with purposeful malice. Elijah's hand moved to the silver crucifix hanging at his chest—a gift from Pope Clement during his ordination fifty years ago. Its familiar weight offered little comfort tonight.

A knock at the door shattered his reverie—three sharp raps that cut through the storm's fury.

"Enter," he called, his voice steady despite the dread coiling in his gut.

The heavy oak door creaked open to reveal Brother Marcus, barely twenty years old, drenched from the storm. The young acolyte's face was pale, his dark eyes wide with a mixture of fear and desperate hope. Water dripped from his black robes, forming a growing puddle at his feet.

"Father Elijah," Marcus began, his voice trembling. "The signs you spoke of—they're happening. The Vatican's communications have gone dark. Rome itself has fallen silent."

Elijah rose slowly, joints protesting as he reached for a sealed letter on his desk. The red wax seal bore the mark of his office—Keeper of the Prophecies, a position unknown to all but the highest ranks of the Church. The letter's contents could shake the foundations of faith itself.

"You were chosen for this moment, Marcus," Elijah said, his voice carrying the weight of divine certainty. "Since the day you arrived here, God has been preparing you."

Marcus stepped forward, his movements hesitant. "But Father, my doubts… How can we be sure? The world is changing so fast. What if we're wrong? What if God has abandoned us?"

Elijah's expression softened as he studied the young man's face. He saw his own struggles reflected there—the same questions that had tortured him in his early years of service. His free hand reached out to grip Marcus's shoulder.

"Faith, my son, is not the absence of doubt. It is the courage to act despite it. Remember the words of Saint Augustine: 'Faith is to believe what you do not see; the reward of this faith is to see what you believe.'"

He pressed the letter into Marcus's trembling hands. "This must reach Pope Clement in Rome. Trust no one else. The words within are not just prophecy—they are a key to understanding what comes next."

Lightning flashed again, illuminating the room in stark white. For a moment, Elijah caught sight of something moving in the shadows behind Marcus—a darkness deeper than a natural shadow, bearing hints of golden amber like dying embers.

"Go now," he urged, his voice dropping to a whisper. "May God's speed be with you, and His angels guard your path."

Marcus clutched the letter to his chest, nodded once, and slipped back into the storm-wracked night. Elijah watched him disappear into the darkness, prayers falling from his lips in an ancient rhythm.

When he turned back to his desk, the air had changed. The temperature plummeted, his breath misting in the suddenly frigid air. The candle's flame turned an unnatural blue, casting sickly light across the centuries-old tomes lining the walls.

The whispers returned, but different now—a sound like serpents sliding across stone, like the hiss of souls burning in eternal fire. The shadows in the corner began to coalesce, taking shape with deliberate, hungry purpose.

"Who dares?" Elijah demanded, though he already knew. The prophecies had warned of this too—the Adversary's emissary, come to test his faith.

From the darkness stepped a figure that seemed to defy reality itself. It wore the form of a man, but wrong—too tall, too fluid in its movements. Shadows clung to it like a living shroud, and where its eyes should be, twin points of golden amber burned like ancient embers, steady and impossibly warm in the freezing dark.

"Prophet," it spoke, its voice resonating not through the air but directly in Elijah's mind. "You who claims to speak for the Divine. Tell me—when was the last time He answered?"

The temperature dropped further. Frost began to creep across the ancient manuscripts, threatening centuries of sacred knowledge. Elijah's hands trembled, but his voice remained firm as he began to recite:

"'Though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil—'"

"Save your psalms," the figure interrupted, taking a step forward. Its movement was too smooth, like a liquid shadow. "The first seal is broken. Your warnings come too late. My son already walks among them — and soon he will not merely walk. He will rule."

"Enough!" Elijah's voice cracked like thunder. He stepped forward, his presence charged with undeniable authority. "By the authority of the original name of Yeshua HaMashiach, the one true Messiah, to whom every knee must bow—I command you!" The very air trembled, a force unseen yet unmistakable rippling through the space around him.

The figure recoiled, its form rippling like smoke in the wind. But its laughter—a sound like breaking glass and crumbling stone—filled the room. "Your faith is strong, old man. But faith alone cannot stop what comes."

A gust of impossible wind tore through the room, extinguishing the candle and scattering papers in a whirlwind. When light returned seconds later, the figure was gone. In its place lay a single black feather that seemed to absorb all light that touched it.

Elijah's legs gave out, and he collapsed into his chair. His heart pounded against his ribs like a prisoner seeking escape. With shaking hands, he opened his journal and began to write:

*"The hour is later than we feared. The Adversary's herald came tonight, bearing confirmation of our worst fears. The first seal is broken—not with a bang, but with the quiet death of truth itself. Our world drowns in lies, and in that flood, the Deceiver has found his path to power.*

*"Brother Marcus carries our last hope to Rome. But I fear—God forgive me, I fear it may not be enough. The prophecies speak of seven seals, seven trials through which our faith must endure. Tonight was only the first.*

*"Yet even in this darkness, I cling to what I know: God's light cannot be extinguished. It lives in the hearts of the faithful, in the courage of those who dare to stand against the coming storm. We are not abandoned. We are being tested.*

*"Heaven weeps tonight, not in despair, but in preparation for the battle to come. For when faith is tested by fire, it emerges either destroyed or unbreakable. Lord, grant us the strength to become the latter."*

The storm raged on outside, but its fury seemed distant now. In the darkness of his study, surrounded by the accumulated wisdom of centuries, Father Elijah continued to write. His words, illuminated by the soft glow of a rekindled candle, would either serve as a testament to humanity's final days or as a beacon of hope for those who would fight the darkness to come.

And somewhere in the night, a young man ran through rain-slicked streets, carrying a letter that could change the course of history—if he lived long enough to deliver it.

The clock struck 3 AM, its bells tolling across a city unknowingly perched on the edge of apocalypse. The devil's hour had begun.

---

Marcus ran.

He ran the way men run when the stakes have stripped everything else away—no thought, no prayer, just legs driving against wet cobblestone, lungs burning, the sealed letter pressed hard against his sternum with both arms crossed over it like a shield. The streets of Florence were a ruin of darkness. No streetlights. No screens glowing in shop windows, no headlights sweeping corners, nothing. The blackout had taken it all. What was left was medieval in its absoluteness: lantern glow seeping from shuttered windows, the orange pulse of a candle behind thick glass, the smell of extinguished electricity and rain-soaked stone. Lightning hit somewhere to the west and for half a second the city was a photograph—porticos and archways and empty piazzas in white-silver light, every puddle mirroring the sky—and then the dark came back harder than before.

He didn't know which was worse. The dark or the brief, brutal clarity.

He ran north, hugging the walls of the old city, the cobblestones slick and uneven under his sandals. His robes were soaked through, heavy as a shroud. He had memorized three routes to the via Cassia before Elijah sent him—before tonight was tonight, when routes were something you could look up on a phone—and he cycled through them now like a litany, each street name a bead on a rosary he didn't have time to hold. He told himself he was not afraid. He told himself this so many times it became indistinct from prayer.

He rounded the corner into the Piazza della Repubblica and broke into the open, his sandals slapping through an inch of standing water, and that was when he saw the figure on the far side of the square.

He almost didn't.

There was too much to see in a flooded piazza in a blackout, too much not-light and not-shape, rain coming sideways now and the fountain somewhere unseen still running because water didn't care what had died. But the figure caught his eye for a moment the way certain things do—not because they are alarming, but because they are so completely, precisely not alarming. Still. Unhurried. Moving through the dark the way you move through a room in your own home, when you know every piece of furniture by heart and the dark is simply the dark and not a problem.

Marcus registered it—a man, walking—and then he was past the piazza and into the next street and his mind gave it no more room than that. He had a letter. He had Rome. He had nothing to spare.

---

The boy was eight years old, or maybe nine. He had not slept. None of them had — not really. When the power died in the afternoon the clocks on every wall had gone dark and the phones had gone dark and the city had simply become night without announcement, without the gradual domestic rituals that marked the transition from day to evening to bed. The storm had rolled in and the storm had stayed and time had dissolved into something that was neither day nor night but only dark, continuous and unspecified. His mother had told him to stay inside and he had stayed inside for almost an hour before drifting to the doorway, which he maintained was not the same as going out. He had been watching the piazza because the piazza in a storm was extraordinary, all that rain on the fountain, all that darkness split by lightning, and he had seen the strange man walking and he had watched the strange man walk, and something in the way the man moved—the absolute unhurriedness, the calm that was somehow different from ordinary calm, as though the rain weren't landing on him the way rain landed on other things—pulled the boy one step out of the doorway and into the wet.

"Are you Jesus?"

The figure stopped.

It turned.

The smile came slowly, the way sunrise comes—a gradual disclosure, arriving in stages until it was simply, fully there. It was a good smile. It was a warm smile, human and certain, the smile of someone who has been recognized and is glad of it. The face above the smile was a face two billion people had spent their lives imagining. It was, to the boy, exactly right.

"Why yes," the figure said. "I am Jesus."

Its voice was gentle. There was no performance in it, no persuasion, no edge of the salesman or the fanatic. It stated the thing the way you state your name when someone has already asked—because the question has been asked and the answer is simply the answer.

The boy turned and ran inside.

His sandals left wet prints across the threshold. He was dripping everywhere, dripping on the flagstones, dripping on the edge of the rug his grandmother had brought from Naples, and he was shouting before he had found his breath.

"Mama! Mama! I saw Jesus! In the piazza, there was a man, he said he was—"

His mother was at the table with a candle and three younger children and a pot of something going cold on an unlit stove. She did not look up. She did not know it was three in the morning. She did not know it was any particular time at all — her phone had died at noon, the battery lantern showed her only the table and the children's faces and the door, and time without electricity becomes a different kind of thing, not a sequence but a condition, the same dark hour repeating until it stops. The world had been ending since this morning and she had no power and no news and her husband had not come home from the factory district and she had fed three children cold pasta and told them it was an adventure.

"Of course you did," she said.

The boy stopped. Turned back to the door.

The figure had continued down the street. It did not hurry. It did not look back. It moved through the darkness of Florence at the same pace it had always been moving, through the rain and the dead lights and the empty piazza, heading south toward the river. Moving through the blackout the way a man moves when he has decided that the world now runs on his schedule, because he has always known it would come to this, because he has been patient in ways that require a different kind of time than people have.

The boy stood in the doorway and watched until the figure was gone.

Behind him, his mother asked him to close the door.

---

Marcus ran north. Toward Rome. Carrying a letter.

Behind him, the Entity walked south through the rain.

They did not meet.

---

*End of Chapter 4*
