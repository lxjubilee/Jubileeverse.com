## #1 — The Feast Table of Yahuah: You Have a Seat — 98/100 [Foundational]

**Paleo-Hebrew Analysis:** Shulchan: Shin-Lamed-Chet-Nun — "The fire guided by the shepherd behind the wall, continuing." The table is the shepherd's fire — protected, sustained, and always set. Tehillim 23:5: "You prepare a table before me in the presence of my enemies."

The table is the central image of the shepherd's ministry — and the central image of celebration in Scripture. From Avraham's hospitality to the Passover seder to the marriage supper of the Lamb, the table is where mishpakhah is formed, identity is confirmed, and covenant is celebrated. This subcategory establishes the theological foundation: Yahuah has set a table, your seat is reserved, and the feast is already prepared. The shepherd does not just feed the sheep — the shepherd throws a banquet. The invitation is not "come and work." The invitation is "come and eat."

## #2 — What is Mishpakhah? The Family of Yahuah — 98/100 [Foundational]

**Paleo-Hebrew Analysis:** Mem-Shin-Pey-Chet-Hey: "Water consumed by the mouth, behind the wall, revealed." Mishpakhah is the revealed community behind the protective wall — a family whose identity is spoken (mouth) and protected (wall). Family is not biological accident; it is prophetic declaration.

The Western church uses the word "community." The Hebrew Scriptures use mishpakhah — family. This subcategory establishes the distinction: community is people who share a space; mishpakhah is people who share blood. Through the blood of Yeshua, every believer is grafted into a family — not an organization, not a club, not a network, but a household with a Father, siblings, shared meals, shared inheritance, and shared destiny. The shepherd builds mishpakhah, not membership rolls.

## #3 — Shabbat: The Weekly Celebration — 97/100 [Foundational]

**Paleo-Hebrew Analysis:** Shin-Bet-Tav: "The consuming fire inside the house of the covenant mark." Shabbat is fire resting inside the covenant home — the weekly rhythm where the family gathers around the flame, breaks bread, drinks wine, and remembers that they belong to Yahuah and to each other.

Shabbat is the crown jewel of celebration — a 24-hour feast that arrives every seven days without invitation. This subcategory explores Shabbat as the shepherd's primary tool for building mishpakhah: the Friday evening candle-lighting, the challah, the wine, the blessings over children, the singing, the rest, the unhurried presence. In a world of perpetual hustle, Shabbat is the shepherd saying: "Stop. Sit down. You are home. Let us celebrate that we are alive and that we are together."

## #4 — The Seven Feasts of Yahuah (Sheva Moedim) — 97/100 [Foundational]

**Paleo-Hebrew Analysis:** Moed: Mem-Vav-Ayin-Dalet — "Water nailed to the eye at the door." The moedim are portals — doorways of divine encounter fixed in time. Each feast is a family reunion with the Father at a door He has chosen.

The seven biblical feasts — Pesach, Matzot, Bikkurim, Shavuot, Yom Teruah, Yom Kippur, Sukkot — are Yahuah's annual family calendar. This subcategory provides an overview of all seven moedim as celebrations that the shepherd leads the mishpakhah through each year. These are not optional religious observances — they are commanded family gatherings with the Father. Every feast tells a story, forms identity, creates memory, and builds family bonds that institutional church programs cannot replicate.

## #5 — The Passover Seder: Celebrating Redemption (Pesach) — 96/100 [Foundational]

**Paleo-Hebrew Analysis:** Pesach: Pey-Samekh-Chet — "The mouth supported behind the wall." Pesach is the spoken word of protection behind the threshold — the family gathered behind the blood-stained doorpost, eating in haste, dressed for departure, celebrating freedom before they have even left.

The Passover seder is the most family-centric celebration in Scripture — designed to be experienced around a table with children asking questions. "Why is this night different from all other nights?" This subcategory explores how to celebrate Pesach as a family: the seder plate, the four cups, the matzah, the bitter herbs, the retelling of the Exodus, and the pointing forward to Yeshua's fulfillment. The shepherd leads the seder, and the children remember forever.

## #6 — Sukkot: The Feast of Tabernacles — 96/100 [Foundational]

**Paleo-Hebrew Analysis:** Sukkah: Samekh-Kaf-Hey — "The support of the open palm, revealed." The sukkah (booth) is the revealed open hand — a temporary structure where Yahuah's covering is visible through the gaps. You can see the stars, and they can see you.

Sukkot is the most joyful feast on Yahuah's calendar — eight days of dwelling in temporary booths, eating outdoors, celebrating the harvest, and remembering that Yahuah is the ultimate shelter. This subcategory explores how to build the sukkah, decorate it, eat in it, sleep in it, invite guests (ushpizin), and experience the raw, beautiful, intentionally imperfect celebration of dwelling with Yahuah under open sky. The shepherd leads the family into the sukkah and says: "This flimsy booth is safer than any fortress because Yahuah is the roof."

## #12 — Purim: Celebration of Divine Reversal — 93/100 [Core]

**Paleo-Hebrew Analysis:** Pur: Pey-Vav-Resh — "The mouth nailed to the head." The lot (pur) was cast by the enemy's mouth — but Yahuah nailed it to His own authority. What was spoken against you is overruled by the One who governs all outcomes.

Purim is the most raucous celebration on the Hebrew calendar — costumes, noisemakers, gifts of food (mishloach manot), feasting, reading the Megillah of Esther, and drinking with joy. This subcategory explores Purim as the feast of divine reversal: the enemy's plot becomes the catalyst for deliverance. The shepherd leads the mishpakhah in celebrating the Purim principle — Yahuah specializes in turning the tables. What was meant for evil becomes the occasion for the greatest party of the year.

Chanukah — the Festival of Dedication — celebrates the Maccabees' victory and the miraculous oil in the rededicated Temple. This subcategory explores Chanukah as a family celebration: lighting the chanukiah (one candle each night for eight nights), playing dreidel, eating foods fried in oil (latkes, sufganiyot), giving gifts, and teaching children that the light of Yahuah persists even when the darkness seems overwhelming. The shepherd uses Chanukah to teach the mishpakhah the theology of resilience.
